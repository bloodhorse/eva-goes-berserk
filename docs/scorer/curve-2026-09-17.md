## the permutation test

Best contestant by P@k at n=270: **nemo/card · rank/centred/pca64/C=0.3**. Labels shuffled inside each room 200 times, same pipeline, same folds.

- real P@k: **0.333**
- shuffled: 0.247 ± 0.051
- p = 0.045 (200 permutations)

| temperature 2.2 | 150 | 36 | 24% | 4 |

`meta/facts` in the scoreboard is a scorer given only those two facts plus card length. If it keeps up with the embeddings, the embeddings have learned nothing a lookup table doesn't already know.

**It nearly keeps up.** `meta/facts` reaches P@k 0.321 at n=270 against the best embedding's 0.365, on nothing but "nemo wrote it". Temperature contributes nothing at all — 25% against 24%. Practically the whole of `meta/facts` is the model column.

But the embeddings are not *only* that. Asked to recover `meta.model` from the vectors alone, leave-one-room-out, they do it poorly: nomic 39%, gpt-2 49%, nemo 54%, against 33% chance (`meta/facts` gets 100%, being the answer). So whatever the embeddings have, it is mostly not model identity — it is just not much.

## two paired comparisons, which are tighter than the ± column

The `±` in the scoreboard is the spread across rooms, and rooms differ enormously in how easy they are. Comparing two contestants **room by room on the same folds** cancels that out, and is the honest test.

- **Best contestant against chance.** `nemo/card · rank` at n=270, minus each room's own base rate: **+0.087 ± 0.057** paired standard error — **+1.5 SE**. Not distinguishable from chance.
- **Best contestant against the cheap facts.** `nemo/card · rank` minus `meta/facts · logreg`, same folds: **+0.055 ± 0.097** — **+0.6 SE**. Not distinguishable from a lookup table. Per room the difference runs from −0.50 to +0.60; it beats the cheap facts in four rooms, loses in four, ties in two.

The permutation test above (p = 0.045) is the same question asked the other way, and it should be read with its thumb on the scale: that contestant was chosen as the best of twenty-eight, and one in twenty-eight coming in at p = 0.045 is exactly what chance looks like.

## the instructed reader — an anecdote, kept separate

Two agents, each shown five rooms of bekh's marks and asked to write a **portrait of him reading** (not a rubric — a definition was forbidden), then to pick about eight cards in each of five rooms it had never seen, from `blind_view.py` output only: shuffled, unmarked, model names stripped. The portraits are `eva/scorer/portrait.md` and `portrait-B.md`, the picks `picks-A.json` and `picks-B.json`. Split B was read by a fresh agent, because the agent that did split A had already seen split B's answers.

| | picks | his marks | precision | recall | ★ caught | chance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| instructed, split A | 40 | 28 | **40%** | 57% | 1/4 | 19% |
| fable blind, same five rooms | 26 | 28 | 50% | 46% | 1/4 | 19% |
| instructed, split B | 40 | 46 | **60%** | 52% | 1/7 | 31% |
| fable blind, same five rooms | 38 | 46 | 61% | 50% | 3/7 | 31% |

bekh's bet was that instructions take a reader from 62% to 80%. **They did not.** On each half the instructed reader landed within a point or two of the blind reader on the same rooms, at about twice the room's base rate — and the two halves differ from each other (40% vs 60%) by more than instruction differs from no instruction, because the rooms differ.

Two things make the 60% worse than it looks. Split B's agent reported four picks it made by **recognising a quote from `HANDOFF.md`'s canon** rather than by reading; all four were hits. Without them split B is **56%**, and its single caught star was one of them, leaving 0 of 7. And the blind reader picked fewer cards in split A (26 against 40), so its precision there is measured on a tighter budget than the instructed reader's.

One agent doing this once, on two splits, is an anecdote. It is reported because it was asked for, and it is not evidence.

## verdict

**Can't tell at this size, and the honest summary is that the curve rises but nothing is distinguishable.** Every source's P@k climbs from roughly chance at n=50 to 0.29–0.37 at n=270, and the two hidden-state sources climb most (`nemo/card` 0.262 → 0.365) — that is the shape you would want. But the best contestant sits +1.5 paired SE above chance and +0.6 SE above a scorer that knows only which model wrote the card, and it was picked as the best of twenty-eight. With ten rooms the error bars are ±0.06 on a quantity of 0.09; nothing here separates from anything.

**The stars taught us nothing and could not have.** `●` did not exist for three of the ten rooms, which leaves 11 true `★` in seven rooms; every star column at n=270 is 0.000. Star-specific training needs an order of magnitude more before it is a question.

**Both model readers beat every trained scorer**, on every split — 50–61% against 0.37 — which is worth sitting with. Reading is still doing better than scoring.

**So far**, and only so far: this does not say the features can't see what bekh sees. It says 300 cards cannot tell us either way. The slope is the thing to watch, and it is cheap to keep watching — the marks come from work that happens anyway. The number to watch next month is not the gap to chance but **the gap to `meta/facts`**: the day a source pulls clearly away from "nemo wrote it", something has been learned.
